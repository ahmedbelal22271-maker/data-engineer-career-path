# Viewpoints: Advice to Aspiring Data Engineers

## Overview

This lesson is a "Viewpoints" video (runtime 6:46) in which **several data professionals** share their personal advice for people who want to break into data engineering. Rather than a single narrative, the video stitches together multiple practitioners' perspectives on how to build foundational skills, gain hands-on experience, choose technologies to master, and contribute to the data engineering community. The points below are organized thematically, but every distinct piece of advice, anecdote, and named tool/technology/resource from the original transcript is preserved.

---

## 1. Build a Strong Foundation First

One contributor frames their advice around two core pieces of guidance for aspiring data engineers:

1. **Focus on building a strong foundation.**
   - Data engineering is described as a **relatively new field**, with new technologies emerging every year.
   - While it's important to learn new technologies as they emerge, the recommended starting point is to build a strong foundation in:
     - **SQL**
     - **Python**
     - **Data modeling**
     - **ETL methodologies**

2. **Pay attention to hands-on experience.**
   - The speaker states plainly: *nothing is worth more in data engineering than hands-on experience.*
   - It might seem hard to get hands-on experience without a full-time job or internship, but the speaker argues **it shouldn't be that hard**, because:
     - The data engineering world has **lots of open-source tools** (referred to as "framing tools" in the transcript).
     - There is an **enormous amount of online learning material** supporting those tools.
     - These resources can be leveraged for **personal projects** and practice.
   - Benefits of hands-on experience called out specifically:
     - It helps a resume **stand out in the candidate pool**.
     - It enables a candidate to **tackle interview questions** more effectively.

---

## 2. Practice Through Real Projects (and a DBA Anecdote)

A second contributor's core advice is: **practice — find things you can do that give you practice in the area.**

### Anecdote: "Break My Database"

> One of the speaker's favorite junior DBAs they worked with used to bring his laptop — with a VM on it — to the speaker and say, *"hey, break my database."* The speaker would then come up with every different way they had seen a database break and try to simulate it on his laptop, and the junior DBA would come up with the solutions. The speaker notes that this junior DBA became a **great troubleshooter** as a result, because he had encountered, through this exercise, many of the same issues that took the speaker **years of experience** to actually run into.

### Other Ways to Practice

- **Build your own project.**
- **Build a database for something in your life** (i.e., a personal, real-world use case).
- **Get involved with a professional organization** — described as a great way to connect with others working in the field and to learn from them.

---

## 3. Learn Database Internals and a Layered Set of Programming Languages

A contributor frames the most important early skill as:

> "Learn database internals. If you understand the data structures that power these data sources, that's half the battle of the data engineer."

This same contributor recommends learning **SQL** plus a deliberate mix of programming language paradigms:

| Language Type | Recommended Examples |
|---|---|
| Procedural language | Shell Scripting, PL/SQL, or Perl |
| Object-oriented language | Java or Python |
| Functional language | Scala |

### Sharing Knowledge

Once a learner has gained some expertise, the advice is to **share knowledge with peers in the community**, specifically by:
- Sharing code in **GitHub public repositories**
- Writing articles on **LinkedIn**
- Creating a **personal blog**

Additional advice from this contributor:
- **Work extremely hard while relatively young.**
- **Stay curious.**
- The speaker asserts that following this advice should remove most obstacles to getting hired, noting that **data engineers are in high demand**.

---

## 4. Giving Back to the Community — The Blogger's Perspective

Another contributor centers their advice on **sharing knowledge and giving back**:

- This speaker self-identifies as **"a Blogger"**, writing extensively about **Db2** and data topics in general at their blog, **DataGeek.blog**.
- Key point: even with as little as **six months** of experience with something, a person may know more about a specific aspect of it than almost anyone else.
- Conclusion drawn from this: **beginner blog entries are great** — and it's important, early in a data engineering journey, to share knowledge and give back to the data engineering community.

---

## 5. Embracing the Breadth of Data Engineering Skills

A final contributor frames data engineering as **a lot of skills — "a lot of width."** Mastering this breadth all at once "can be very overwhelming," so the advice is to tackle it in a deliberate sequence:

1. **Master one RDBMS** — any relational database management system the learner has access to and is comfortable with.
2. **Master at least one NoSQL database** — examples given: **MongoDB**, **Cassandra**, **Neo4J**, **React**, or **Radius**. *(Note: as transcribed; some of these names — e.g., "React" and "Radius" — appear to be transcription artifacts of NoSQL database names such as Redis, and are preserved here verbatim from the source transcript.)*
3. **Understand web scraping concepts** and begin learning them.
4. **Understand and prepare to work with APIs** — described as "a way with which you can interact with other systems seamlessly," and noted as an expectation for the future.

### Building and Demonstrating Skills

- **Build the right skills** as the first priority.
- **Get credentials and certificates.**
- **Demonstrate expertise** in a technology or product that:
  - You enjoy working with, **and**
  - Is in demand.
- Concrete ways to demonstrate expertise:
  - Write **blog posts**
  - Write **articles**
  - Create **YouTube videos**
  - Create **screencasts and demos**
- You **don't have to be an expert** to create this content — one of the best ways to learn something is to try to teach it to others.

### Community Involvement

- **Join a local networking group or meetup** — not just to learn, but to meet others in the field and share insights and experiences.

### Continuous Learning

- Like other technology fields, **data engineering is continuously evolving**.
- Learning should be treated as a **continuous, lifelong process**.
- Keep up with **new technologies and approaches** to working with data.

The video closes with a wish for success to anyone on the journey to becoming a data engineer.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Foundation first:** SQL, Python, data modeling, and ETL methodologies are the recommended starting point before chasing every new technology.
- **Hands-on experience is irreplaceable** — and accessible via open-source tools, online learning materials, and personal projects, even without a full-time role.
- **Deliberate practice matters:** building your own projects, "breaking" databases to learn troubleshooting, and joining professional organizations all build real skill.
- **Understand database internals** and learn one language from each paradigm: procedural (Shell Scripting/PL/SQL/Perl), object-oriented (Java/Python), and functional (Scala).
- **Give back early:** share code on GitHub, write on LinkedIn or a personal blog — even beginner-level content has value, sometimes after as little as six months of experience.
- **Master breadth deliberately:** one RDBMS, one NoSQL database, web scraping concepts, and API fluency, rather than trying to learn everything simultaneously.
- **Demonstrate and teach:** credentials and certificates help, but so do blog posts, articles, videos, screencasts, and demos — teaching is itself a learning tool.
- **Stay engaged with community:** local networking groups and meetups provide both learning and relationship-building opportunities.
- **Treat learning as continuous:** the field evolves constantly, and lifelong learning is part of the job.
