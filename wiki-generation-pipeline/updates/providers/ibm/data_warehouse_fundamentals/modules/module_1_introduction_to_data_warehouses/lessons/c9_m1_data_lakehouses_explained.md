# Data Lakehouses Explained

Last week I'm having dinner at this restaurant and I'm looking around. The place is packed, everyone's getting their orders on time and I couldn't help but think about the logistics that go into a restaurant, turning raw ingredients into these delicious meals. Let's think about this for a minute. In a commercial kitchen, we have raw ingredients being delivered by trucks to our loading dock on large palettes. Truck comes in to the loading dock, they drop off the palette and the truck is back out on the road to deliver more ingredients to other restaurants. That's the easy part. Now, we actually have to unwrap this palette and process it.

## The Restaurant Analogy — Ingredient Processing

We have to sort everything on it. We have to label all of our ingredients. Then we also have to make sure that each item is routed to the correct storage area. These things could be going into a pantry for dry goods, or it could also be going into large walk-in fridges and freezers for things like fresh vegetables and meats, and we also have to organize those storage areas. We've got to make sure that ingredients that are expiring first are used first. We've got to make sure certain ingredients are separated from one another for contamination reasons. We also have to make sure that certain ingredients hit a very certain temperature, also for food safety.

By the way, we need to do all of this as quickly as possible. To minimize things like food waste, to minimize spoilage that we could see from the ingredients just sitting on the truck or on a pallet. Without this process, the cooks in the kitchen can't really do their job as effectively or safely. They'd be spending a lot of their time just looking for ingredients and less time actually cooking and serving out meals to their customers.

## From Kitchen to Data — The Organizational Data Flow

What does this have to do with data? Well, if we think about it, this very same process also exists within data architectures of organizations. You've got all sorts of different data coming into your organization from different sources, such as in different Cloud environments, different operational applications. Now we even have social media data. All this is coming into our organization just like a kitchen has ingredients coming from different suppliers. [ENRICHED: definition — Data sources in modern organizations include structured data from operational databases (ERP, CRM systems), semi-structured data from APIs and JSON logs, unstructured data from social media, emails, and documents, and streaming data from IoT sensors and real-time applications. The diversity of these sources is what makes data lakes necessary — traditional warehouses cannot ingest all these formats without transformation. Source: Bacancy Technology] [Source: https://www.bacancytechnology.com/blog/data-warehouse-vs-data-lake-vs-data-lakehouse]

## Data Lakes — The Loading Dock

Constantly have data coming in, we need a quick place to dump all different types of data in different formats for later use. We have data lakes. Now these lakes allow us to cheaply and quickly capture raw, structured and unstructured and even semi-structured data. [ENRICHED: definition — A data lake is a centralized storage repository that holds raw data in its native format — structured, semi-structured, and unstructured — at low cost on object storage like Amazon S3, Google Cloud Storage, or Azure Data Lake Storage. It uses schema-on-read, meaning structure is applied at query time rather than on ingestion. Data lakes require strong governance to avoid becoming disorganized "data swamps." Source: dataskew.io] [Source: https://dataskew.io/blog/data-warehouse-vs-data-lake/]

Now just like in the kitchen, we're not really cooking on the loading dock. Now maybe I can put a tiny grill there if I really wanted to, but we have to organize and transform this data from its raw state into something that's usable for the insights and analytics that are business wants to generate.

## Enterprise Data Warehouses — The Pantry and Freezer

We have enterprise data warehouses or EDW's, where data is loaded in, sometimes from a data lake, but sometimes from other sources like operational applications and it's optimized and organized to run very specific analytical tasks. Now this could be powering different business intelligence or BI workloads such as building dashboards and reports. Or it could be feeding into other analytical tools. Just like our pantries and freezers, data in the warehouse is cleaned, organized, governed, and should be trusted for integrity. [ENRICHED: ecosystem — Enterprise Data Warehouses (EDWs) use schema-on-write: data must conform to a predefined structure before loading. This ensures consistency and fast query performance but limits flexibility. Leading cloud EDWs include Snowflake, Google BigQuery, Amazon Redshift, and Azure Synapse Analytics. The ETL (Extract, Transform, Load) process transforms raw data before it enters the warehouse, which is why warehouse data is "cleaned and organized" as the video states. Source: Bacancy Technology] [Source: https://www.bacancytechnology.com/blog/data-warehouse-vs-data-lake-vs-data-lakehouse]

## Challenges of the Data Lake and Warehouse Approach

### Data Lake Challenges — The Data Swamp Problem

What are some of the challenges that we see in this approach? Well, as we said, data lakes are really awesome to capture tons of data in a cost-effective way, but we run into challenges with data governance and data quality. A lot of times these data lakes can become data swamps. This happens when there's a lot of duplicate, inaccurate, or incomplete data, making it difficult to track and manage assets. [ENRICHED: ecosystem — The "data swamp" phenomenon is well-documented. A 2026 academic study spanning 15 years of data lake implementations found that 60-85% of big data projects fail (Gartner), and Gartner warned as early as 2014 that "through 2018, 80% of data lakes will not include effective metadata management capabilities, making them inefficient." The study identifies seven anti-patterns: ingesting without purpose, avoiding schema design, treating governance as an afterthought, technology worship, the democratization illusion, the skills mirage, and the cost delusion. Data swamps form when lakes fill with undocumented, unvalidated content that nobody understands, maintains, or uses. Source: arXiv] [Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08266v1]

If you think about it, what happens when that data becomes stale? Well, it loses its value and creating insights. The same way that ingredients go bad over time in our restaurant if we don't use them. [ENRICHED: performance context — Data freshness is critical: stale data leads to incorrect business decisions. Studies show that the average annual cost of poor data quality is $12.9 million (Gartner estimate). Organizations report that analysts spend 35-40% of their time finding and cleaning data rather than analyzing it when data lakes lack proper governance and freshness monitoring. Source: arXiv] [Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08266v1]

Data lakes also have challenges with query performance since they're not built and optimized to handle the complex analytical queries. It can sometimes be tough to get insights out of lakes directly. [ENRICHED: performance context — Data lakes without optimization layers scan entire file sets without statistics, and performance degrades as data volume grows. Without ACID transactions, concurrent writes produce partial reads and failed writes leave corrupt partial files. Without schema enforcement at write time, schema drift occurs as different pipeline versions write incompatible schemas silently. These limitations are what drove the development of lakehouse architecture. Source: askantech.com] [Source: https://www.askantech.com/data-warehouse-vs-data-lake-vs-data-lakehouse-analytics-infrastructure-2026/]

### Data Warehouse Challenges — Cost and Flexibility Limits

Let's take a look at the data warehouse now. Now these are really great at query performance. They're exceptional, but they can come at a high cost. Just like those big freezers can be very costly to run, we can't put everything into a data warehouse. Now they can be better optimized to maintain data governance and quality, but they have limited support for semi-structured and unstructured data sources. By the way, the ones that are growing the most, that are coming into our organization. They can also sometimes be too slow for certain types of applications that require the freshest data because it takes time to sort, clean and load data into the warehouse. [ENRICHED: ecosystem — Data warehouse costs are higher because compute and storage are coupled (proprietary columnar storage), and data must be transformed via ETL before loading. Semi-structured data (JSON, XML) and unstructured data (images, text, video) — which represent the fastest-growing data types — require flattening or conversion before warehouse ingestion, losing inherent structure. For real-time applications (fraud detection, recommendation engines), the ETL delay makes warehouses impractical. Source: askantech.com] [Source: https://www.askantech.com/data-warehouse-vs-data-lake-vs-data-lakehouse-analytics-infrastructure-2026/]

## The Data Lakehouse — Best of Both Worlds

What do we do here? Well, developers took a step back and said, "Hey, let's take the best of both data lakes and data warehouses and combine them into a new technology called the data lake house." [ENRICHED: definition — A data lakehouse is a modern data architecture that combines the low-cost, flexible storage of a data lake with the performance, ACID transactions, and governance features of a data warehouse. It is enabled by open table format layers like Delta Lake, Apache Iceberg, and Apache Hudi, which sit on top of object storage and provide schema enforcement, time travel, and reliable concurrent access. The term "lakehouse" was coined by Databricks in 2020, and by 2026 over 50% of organizations have implemented lakehouse patterns. Source: Databricks, dataskew.io] [Source: https://www.databricks.com/blog/data-lakes-vs-data-warehouses-what-your-organization-needs-know]

[ENRICHED: clarification — "Why not just use a plain warehouse?" The video's restaurant analogy doesn't make this case strongly enough, so here is the full technical justification for why a warehouse alone is insufficient:

**1. Data variety — warehouses can only handle structured data.**
Data warehouses use schema-on-write: every record must match a predefined structure before loading. This works for structured data (rows and columns from relational databases), but the fastest-growing data types in modern organizations are semi-structured (JSON API responses, XML logs, clickstream events) and unstructured (images, video, audio, emails, social media posts, IoT sensor streams). A warehouse cannot efficiently store these — you must flatten, convert, or discard structure during ETL, losing valuable information. A lakehouse stores all data types natively in open formats (Parquet, JSON, Avro) without forced conversion. Source: datadrivendaily.com, Bacancy Technology] [Source: https://datadrivendaily.com/data-lakehouse-explained]

**2. Cost — warehouses are expensive at scale.**
Data warehouses couple compute and storage on proprietary platforms. Snowflake and BigQuery bills can reach $200K-500K/year for mid-size companies on warehouse compute alone. Lakehouses decouple storage (cheap cloud object storage at ~$23/TB/month) from compute (pay only for queries you run), allowing independent scaling. Organizations report 60-70% storage cost reductions after moving from warehouse-only to lakehouse. Source: datadrivendaily.com] [Source: https://datadrivendaily.com/data-lakehouse-explained]

**3. ML/AI workloads need raw data, not pre-transformed data.**
Data scientists need access to raw, granular, unmodified data for feature engineering and model training. Warehouses force ETL before loading, which means data must be exported out of the warehouse for ML training — creating data duplication, pipeline complexity, and consistency risk. Lakehouses serve both BI (on curated data) and ML (on raw data) from the same storage layer, eliminating the need to maintain separate systems. Source: datadrivendaily.com, DataCamp] [Source: https://www.datacamp.com/blog/data-lakehouse-vs-data-warehouse]

**4. Real-time analytics — warehouses are batch-oriented.**
Warehouse ETL pipelines are inherently batch-oriented: data is sorted, cleaned, and loaded on a schedule (hourly, daily). Applications requiring the freshest data (fraud detection, recommendation engines, real-time dashboards) cannot wait for the next ETL cycle. Lakehouses support streaming ingestion natively (Kafka, Spark Streaming), enabling real-time analytics alongside historical processing on the same data. Source: DataCamp, VE3] [Source: https://www.datacamp.com/blog/data-lakehouse-vs-data-warehouse]

**5. Vendor lock-in — warehouses use proprietary formats.**
Data warehouse storage formats are vendor-specific and proprietary. Migrating from one warehouse to another (e.g., from Redshift to Snowflake) requires data export, reformatting, and pipeline rebuilds — a process that can take months and cost millions. Lakehouses use open table formats (Delta Lake, Iceberg, Hudi) on open file formats (Parquet), meaning the same data can be read by multiple compute engines (Spark, Trino, Flink) and moved between cloud providers without reformatting. Source: Starburst, Lucent Innovation] [Source: https://www.starburst.io/blog/data-warehouse-data-lake-data-lakehouse/]

**Bottom line:** A warehouse is the right choice when your workloads are purely structured SQL analytics with no ML, no unstructured data, and no real-time requirements. But most modern organizations have all three. The lakehouse exists because the warehouse's limitations — cost, data type rigidity, ML incompatibility, batch-only processing, and vendor lock-in — became too painful as data volumes grew and AI/ML became a strategic priority. Source: datadrivendaily.com, Databricks] [Source: https://datadrivendaily.com/data-lakehouse-explained]

We get the flexibility and we get the cost-effectiveness of a data lake and we get the performance and structure of a data warehouse. [ENRICHED: ecosystem — The key innovation enabling lakehouses is ACID (Atomicity, Consistency, Isolation, Durability) transactions on object storage. Traditional data lakes on S3/GCS/ADLS lack ACID guarantees, meaning concurrent writes can corrupt data and there's no rollback capability. Open table formats (Delta Lake, Iceberg, Hudi) add a transactional metadata layer that provides ACID transactions, schema enforcement, time travel (querying historical versions), and compaction (merging small files for performance). This is what makes "warehouse-like reliability on lake-level storage" possible. Source: Databricks] [Source: https://docs.databricks.com/aws/en/lakehouse/acid]

## Lakehouse Value Proposition

We'll talk more specifically about the architecture of a data lake house in a future video, but from a value point of view, the lake house lets us store data from the exploding number of new sources in a low-cost way and then leverages built-in data management and governance leaders to allow us to power both business intelligence and high performance machine learning workloads quickly. [ENRICHED: ecosystem — The lakehouse eliminates the most painful costs of running both a warehouse and a lake in parallel: (1) Data duplication — with a two-system architecture, data gets copied from the lake into the warehouse for analytics, creating consistency risk, additional storage cost, and pipeline complexity. The lakehouse serves both workloads from the same data. (2) Governance fragmentation — governance rules applied differently in the lake vs. the warehouse create compliance gaps. The lakehouse centralizes governance. (3) Talent overhead — teams need separate skills for lake and warehouse platforms. The lakehouse unifies the tooling. Organizations report 35-40% total cost of ownership reductions compared to maintaining separate lake and warehouse infrastructures. Source: Lucent Innovation, arXiv] [Source: https://www.lucentinnovation.com/resources/it-insights/data-warehouse-vs-data-lake-vs-lakehouse]

## Use Cases for Lakehouses

There are plenty of ways that we can start using a lake house. We can modernize our existing data lakes. We can compliment our data warehouses to support some of these new types of AI and machine learning driven workloads. But we'll also talk about that in the future video. [ENRICHED: ecosystem — In 2026, the dominant lakehouse platforms are Databricks (Delta Lake + Unity Catalog), Snowflake (Snowflake Open Catalog + Iceberg), and Microsoft Fabric (OneLake + Delta Lake). The medallion architecture (Bronze → Silver → Gold layers) is the standard pattern for organizing data refinement in a lakehouse: Bronze stores raw ingested data, Silver applies cleaning and standardization, and Gold contains business-level aggregated datasets ready for BI and ML. Source: AppScale Blog, Taqom] [Source: https://appscale.blog/en/blog/data-lakehouse-architecture-iceberg-delta-medallion-2026]
